The sponge was already clean. All the agent had to do was put it on a cart. But its harness kept telling it to go back and clean the sponge. The agent ran out of turns. Without that harness, the same model finished in **14 turns**.

That failure captures what we set out to fix with **JoMoHa**: a model and its harness can each learn, yet get worse at working together.

[Read the paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11499) · [Explore the code](https://github.com/xudongwu-0/coevolve)

## Why taking turns can make things worse

A harness is the code around a model that shapes what it sees, what it keeps track of, and how it interacts with the environment. The obvious way to improve both is to take turns: freeze the harness and train the model, then freeze the model and train the harness. In our long-phase alternating run on ALFWorld, success initially rose from **83.0% to 90.5%**, then fell to **31.5%**.

We tested saved model checkpoints with and without the same old harness and found two problems:

- **The old harness got in the way more often as the model changed.** In the sponge task, the harness missed an update from the environment, treated cleaning as unfinished, and sent the model back to a step it had already completed.
- **The model itself also got worse.** Removing the harness did not restore the earlier checkpoint's performance. A mismatched harness doesn't just affect what the model does at test time; it also shapes the experience the model learns from.

## One task, one reward, two critics

In JoMoHa, an **Engineer** writes an executable harness and a **Solver** uses it to act. We train both policies with reinforcement learning from the **same task attempt**: the Engineer learns to write a better harness for the Solver it actually worked with, and the Solver learns from acting under that harness. The system learns to write its own harness rather than relying on a fixed external editor.

But sharing a reward doesn't make it equally informative for both roles. A failed run could reflect poor guidance from the harness, a bad decision by the Solver, or a mismatch between the two. A **critic** gives each policy a baseline for judging whether the outcome was better or worse than expected. JoMoHa keeps the two critics separate, but gives each a short, role-specific summary of the collaboration, including the task, training version, and execution conditions. The Engineer's critic estimates returns in light of the Solver it is writing for; the Solver's critic estimates returns in light of the harness it is working under. This context goes to the critics during training, not to the policies making decisions.

The point isn't to assign exact blame after a failure. It's to give each learner a more useful comparison: **how well did this run go given the conditions the two policies were working under?** We also normalize returns separately for each role, adjust policy-update weights using the partner's recorded trajectory, and give the Solver extra rounds to adapt to the harnesses it encounters.

## What changed when we trained them together?

On ALFWorld, training both policies from the same runs without the coordination mechanisms reached **88.5%**. Adding critic context alone raised that to **92.5%**, the largest gain among the individual additions. Combining all four mechanisms reached **97.0%**. A separate control that kept the Engineer frozen while still sampling fresh harnesses reached **90.5%** at the same reported training step.

JoMoHa led the methods we compared on all three benchmarks: **97.0%** on ALFWorld, **45.8%** on WebShop, and **68.0%** on DBBench. That's **70.3% average task success**, **7.2 percentage points above WHALE**.

The takeaway: **don't just train a better model and a better harness. Train them to work better together.**
