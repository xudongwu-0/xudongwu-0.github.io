## Abstract

When both the model weights and the harness can change, optimizing them in turn can make them less compatible. We diagnose two failures: an old harness can increasingly interfere with execution, and the Solver can regress even when evaluated without it. **JoMoHa** is, to our knowledge, the first reinforcement-learning framework to jointly train a policy that writes executable harnesses and a policy that acts under them. Both learn from the same task attempt, with separate critics that account for the conditions under which they worked together. JoMoHa reaches **97.0%** on ALFWorld and averages **70.3%** across three benchmarks.

## What goes wrong when we alternate?

The sponge was already clean. All the Solver had to do was put it on a cart. But the harness, the code that tracks progress and guides the model through the task, had missed the update. It kept sending the Solver back to clean the sponge. The run hit the **50-turn limit**. Take the harness away and the same Solver finished in **14 turns**.

That was one failure in a long-phase alternating run on ALFWorld. We trained the Solver with the harness fixed, then trained the harness writer with the Solver fixed. At first, taking turns looked like it was working:

Was this just a bad harness? We tested earlier and later Solver checkpoints with and without the **same** learned harness. The old harness interfered more with the later Solver, but the later Solver also did worse on its own. This is what makes training the two components hard to separate: the harness influences not only how the model acts, but also what it experiences during training.

## What should the two policies learn from each other?

Our starting point was to update both policies from the **same task attempt**. The Engineer learns to write harnesses for the Solver it actually worked with, and the Solver learns from acting under the code the Engineer actually wrote. The harness writer itself learns from execution feedback rather than staying fixed.

<details class="jomoha-walkthrough">
  <summary>Play: Follow one JoMoHa training round</summary>
  <ol class="jomoha-steps">
    <li><strong>Engineer writes</strong> an executable harness for the task.</li>
    <li><strong>Solver acts</strong> under that harness and receives a task reward.</li>
    <li><strong>Two critics estimate</strong> what each role could expect under those collaboration conditions.</li>
    <li><strong>Both policies learn</strong> from the same task reward, each updating from the tokens it generated.</li>
  </ol>
</details>

The subtle part is choosing what to compare that reward against. Back to the sponge: a failed run tells us the pair didn't finish. It doesn't tell us whether the Engineer wrote unhelpful code or the Solver made a poor choice despite good guidance. We give each policy its own **critic**. Each estimates a baseline using the policy's own history plus a short summary of the task, training version, and execution conditions. The critics don't share weights, and the extra context doesn't go to the policies. What they share is the task attempt: each can ask whether its outcome was better or worse than expected *given the conditions under which the pair worked*.

The other design choices address different parts of the same training problem. **Separate return normalization** accounts for the different scales of writing one program and acting over many turns. **Partner-trajectory weighting** adjusts how much a recorded attempt influences an update as the other policy changes. **Extra Solver updates** give it time to adapt to sampled harnesses without another change to the Engineer's weights.

## Does coordination improve the pair?

On ALFWorld, joint training without those four mechanisms reached **88.5%**. Adding critic context alone brought it to **92.5%**, the largest gain among the individual additions. The full method reached **97.0%**.

We also tested whether the learned harness itself got better, not just the Solver. With the later Solver's weights held fixed, swapping the initial harness for the later one raised success from **92.5% to 96.5%** in the cross-checkpoint evaluation. In the main comparison, JoMoHa led the evaluated methods on ALFWorld, WebShop, and DBBench, averaging **70.3%**, **7.2 percentage points above WHALE**.
