A harness can work well for one model checkpoint and get in the way of the next. That makes post-training a coupled problem: the model learns from the experience its harness gives it, while the value of the harness depends on the model using it. How should we train when both can change?

We started by testing whether simply alternating between them was enough. It wasn't in our experiment. That led us to **JoMoHa**, to our knowledge the first reinforcement-learning framework to jointly train a policy that writes executable harnesses and a policy that carries out tasks under them.

[Read the paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11499) · [Explore the code](https://github.com/xudongwu-0/coevolve)

## What goes wrong when we alternate?

In our long-phase alternating run on ALFWorld, success first rose from **83.0% to 90.5%**, then fell to **31.5%**. To understand why, we evaluated saved Solver checkpoints with and without the same learned harness. Two things changed: the old harness interfered more as the Solver changed, and the later Solver performed worse even after we removed the harness.

The distinction matters. In one task, the harness failed to register that a sponge was already clean and kept directing the Solver back to clean it. The Solver hit the **50-turn limit** with the harness but finished in **14 turns** without it. That was a mismatch at execution time. The drop we still saw without the harness points to a separate problem: changing the code at test time cannot recover capabilities the model lost during training.

## What should the two policies learn from each other?

In JoMoHa, the unit of training is **one task attempt**. An **Engineer** writes an executable harness; a **Solver** acts under it. Both update their weights using the task reward from that run, each learning from the tokens it generated. The harness writer itself learns from execution feedback instead of relying on a fixed editor to propose better code.

A shared reward gives the pair a common objective, but it doesn't give either role a good baseline on its own. The Engineer's code is evaluated by whichever Solver runs it, and the Solver's decisions are made under whichever harness it was given. **We use two separate critics, each conditioned on the collaboration.** Each critic estimates the expected return for its role from its own history plus a short summary of the task, training version, and execution conditions. The extra context is for the critics, not the policies. This lets each update compare the outcome against what was expected under those conditions, rather than against a baseline that ignores the partner.

The other design choices address different parts of the same training problem. **Separate return normalization** accounts for the different scales of writing one program and acting over many turns. **Partner-trajectory weighting** adjusts how much a recorded attempt influences an update as the other policy changes. **Extra Solver updates** give it time to adapt to sampled harnesses without another change to the Engineer's weights.

## Does coordination improve the pair?

On ALFWorld, joint training without those four mechanisms reached **88.5%**. Adding critic context alone brought it to **92.5%**, the largest gain among the individual additions. The full method reached **97.0%**.

We also tested whether the learned harness itself got better, not just the Solver. With the later Solver's weights held fixed, swapping the initial harness for the later one raised success from **92.5% to 96.5%** in the cross-checkpoint evaluation. In the main comparison, JoMoHa led the evaluated methods on ALFWorld, WebShop, and DBBench, averaging **70.3%**, **7.2 percentage points above WHALE**.
